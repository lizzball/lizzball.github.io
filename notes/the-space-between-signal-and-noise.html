The space between signal and noise

On why careful measurement, validation and context should come before confident clinical claims.

FIELD NOTE 01METHODS & INTERPRETATIONOCTOBER 2026

In translational science, the most interesting question isn't always whether two groups differ. Often, it's whether the difference can survive an encounter with the world outside the original dataset.

A biomarker can look persuasive in a histogram. A model can report a strong area under the ROC curve. Neither tells us whether an assay is reproducible, whether preprocessing separated one study site from another, or whether the classification will work on independent patients.

Measurement comes first

Before fitting a model, there is the specimen: how it was collected, stored and processed. There is the assay: its detection limits, variability, controls and normalization. And there is the sampling structure: who contributed data, how groups were recruited and whether the rows are truly independent.

Technical repeatability and biological relevance are related but distinct. A stable measurement can be biologically uninformative; an interesting molecule can be measured too inconsistently to support a useful test.

Validation is a design choice

Validation isn't an afterthought to be added once a model performs well. It determines what the reported performance actually means. If two samples from the same patient occur in training and testing, the model may be learning a person's fingerprint rather than transferable disease biology. If feature selection happens before splitting, information can leak into the test folds.

These choices are especially consequential in small datasets, where a handful of observations can shift the result substantially.

Uncertainty belongs in the story

Interpreting an apparent signal responsibly requires more than one score. It means inspecting errors, thresholds, subgroup behaviour, calibration, study design and potential confounding. It also means being willing to find that the data don't yet support the claim we hoped to make.

That is not a failed result. It is part of useful science: knowing when a finding is exploratory, which assumptions it depends on and what evidence would make the next conclusion stronger.

An original methodological reflection. Not a report of new experimental results or a clinical recommendation.
